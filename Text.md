**STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Under *28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)*, a federal court may not grant habeas relief on any claim "adjudicated on the merits" in state court unless the state court's adjudication "(1) resulted in a decision that was contrary to, or involved an unreasonable application of, clearly established Federal law, as determined by the Supreme Court of the United States; or (2) resulted in a decision that was based on an unreasonable determination of the facts in light of the evidence presented in the State court proceeding."

A state-court decision is "contrary to" clearly established federal law if it applies a rule that contradicts governing Supreme Court precedent or reaches a different result on materially indistinguishable facts. A decision involves an "unreasonable application" of clearly established federal law if the state court identifies the correct governing legal principle but unreasonably applies it to the facts of the case. *Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362, 405–13 (2000)*. "Clearly established Federal law" means the holdings of the Supreme Court as of the time of the relevant state-court decision. *Greene v. Fisher, 565 U.S. 34, 38 (2011)*. The standard is difficult to meet by design: a state prisoner must show that the state court's ruling was "so lacking in justification that there was an error well understood and comprehended in existing law beyond any possibility for fairminded disagreement." *Harrington v. Richter, 562 U.S. 86, 103 (2011)*. Review under *§ 2254(d)(1)* is generally limited to the record that was before the state court. *Cullen v. Pinholster, 563 U.S. 170, 181 (2011)*. A state court's factual findings are presumed correct, and a petitioner challenging them under *§ 2254(d)(2)* bears the burden of rebutting that presumption by clear and convincing evidence. *28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(1)*; *Rice v. Collins, 546 U.S. 333, 338–39 (2006)*.

 STATEMENT ON DEFERENCE

Before reaching the merits, Petitioner establishes that none of the state court decisions in this post-conviction proceeding are entitled to deference under *28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)*.

AEDPA deference does not attach where a state court fails to issue a reasoned adjudication on the merits, bases its decision on an unreasonable determination of the facts, or operates under a fundamental breakdown of due process. Because Oklahoma's post-conviction process was structurally defective and failed to adjudicate the claims actually presented, this Court must review all substantive grounds de novo.

**A. STRUCTURAL MISCHARACTERIZATION OF THE CLAIMS (*§ 2254(d)(2)*):** Petitioner's October 7, 2025 Application for Post-Conviction Relief raised only two cognizable claims: (1) Ineffective Assistance of Appellate Counsel (IAAC) under *Strickland v. Every alleged trial error—prosecutorial misconduct, destruction of evidence, false testimony, and defective instructions—was pled exclusively as evidence establishing appellate counsel's deficient performance for omitting these reversible issues on direct appeal.

An IAAC claim is cognizable for the first time on post-conviction review (*Logan v. State, 2013 OK CR 2, ¶ 5, 293 P.3d 969, 973*) and cannot be barred by waiver or res judicata. The state courts, however, manufactured a strawman by treating the underlying evidentiary errors as freestanding claims and dismissing them as procedurally defaulted.

By mischaracterizing the face of the pleading to construct a procedural bar, the state courts failed to adjudicate the actual IAAC claim presented. This misreading constitutes an "unreasonable determination of the facts in light of the evidence presented" under *28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(2)*, stripping the decision of AEDPA deference (*Rice v. **B. VERBATIM ADOPTION OF PROSECUTION'S BRIEF WITHOUT INDEPENDENT REVIEW:** On February 13, 2026, the district court denied relief in an order copied near-verbatim from the State's response. Under *Wilson v. Sellers, 138 S. Ct. 1188, 1192 (2018)*, this Court "looks through" summary affirmances to the last reasoned state-court decision. A rubber-stamped adoption of a prosecutor's brief is not a reasoned decision (*Rudenko v. Costello, 286 F.3d 51, 62–63 (2d Cir. 2002)*; *Pederson v. State, 649 N.W.2d 161, 167 (Minn. 2002)*). Where a state court fails to consider the supporting facts, it has not adjudicated the claim on the merits (*Wilson v. Workman, 577 F.3d 1284, 1295 (10th Cir. 2009)*).

**I. Governing Law:** The Supreme Court has assumed that a "truly persuasive demonstration of 'actual innocence'" in an extraordinary case would independently warrant habeas relief. *House v. Bell, 547 U.S. 518, 554–55 (2006)*; *McQuiggin v. Perkins, 569 U.S. 383, 392 (2013)*. Separately, the identical evidentiary showing satisfies the actual-innocence gateway recognized in *Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298, 327 (1995)*: a petitioner passes through this gateway by showing "it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have convicted him in light of the new evidence," unlocking every other constitutional claim in this petition from procedural default. *Id.* at 320–21; *Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 623 (1998)*.

Both doctrines require "a holistic judgment about all the evidence, old and new, admissible and inadmissible." *Fontenot v. Crow, 4 F.4th 982, 1031–32 (10th Cir. 2021)* (applying *House,* 547 U.S. at 538). *Fontenot* also holds that evidence is "new" if it was not presented to the jury, regardless of whether it existed at trial or why trial counsel failed to use it. *Id.* at 1032–35. This "newly presented" standard matters here, because none of the evidence below was hidden or unavailable — it existed at the time of trial and was simply never shown to the jury.

She confirmed she had discussed the case with her father and stepmother that very morning:

  - A. Yes, only a little bit, though" (Ex. C9 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 26),

and confirmed they had "supported" her through the hearing. Immediately before testifying under oath, she had engaged in documented preparation with the adults residing with her.

This expansion occurred immediately after an escalating hearing that was preceded by a prep session with the lead prosecutor.

Before each of the first three formal proceedings, there is a documented conversation between the complainant and an adult positioned to influence the account. After every single conversation, the account altered. Between Police Report and Forensic Interview: At least five documented adult conversations; State's own expert admits the child appeared prepped; account changes in location, act, and severity. Between Forensic Interview and Protective Order Hearing: Minimum of two adult conversations (Detective; father and stepmother the morning of court); account reverts from completed act back to an attempt. Between Protective Order Hearing and Preliminary Hearing: Documented therapy sessions and a private meeting with the lead prosecutor the day before court; account escalates to full digital penetration for the first time.

Progressive escalation of an account across successive, officially conducted interviews is a recognized scientific indicator of unreliability and fabricative contamination. *United States v. Cline, 349 F.3d 1276, 1287 (10th Cir. 2003)*. Here, the pattern does not merely track the natural passage of time — it tracks specific, documented contacts with specific adults at identifiable moments, culminating in the largest escalation occurring less than 24 hours after a private meeting with the lead prosecutor. Combined with the State forensic interviewer's sworn admission that the child "clearly had been told or informed as to what to expect," this sequence provides substantive proof of structural contamination.

**V. Documentary Evidence Proves Petitioner Was Not Physically Present During the Only Window Ever Alleged:** The Petitioner signed an independent lease for a new residence on September 4, 2018. (Ex. J1) A photograph dated September 8, 2018 shows a moving truck and trailer in front of the new residence (a distinct blue house) (Ex. J4–J5) Petitioner did not return to the family home until December 30, 2018 (Ex. J6) — five days after the outer boundary of the window fixed by the police report. Furthermore, the complainant departed for Arizona on December 22, 2018 (Ex. J7) and did not return until January 5, 2019. (Ex. J8) Tiffany Norton confirmed under oath at trial that Petitioner "never spent the night" during this separation. (Ex. F13 - Trial Tr. II at 87)

Laid side-by-side using only dates supplied by the State's own witnesses and contemporaneous objective documents, there is zero physical overlap between September 8, 2018 and January 5, 2019 where Petitioner and the complainant were both present in that home under the circumstances described. Yet this exact timeframe is the only one ever identified by the complainant.

The State cannot defeat this actual innocence showing by falling back on its amended 21-month charging window (September 2017 – June 2019) or standard "on or about" jury instructions for two independent reasons:

1. As established in Section III, the complainant did not give a vague or floating timeframe; she bound her allegations to explicit temporal anchors ("5th grade," "middle of 5th grade," "cold out," "before Christmas," "a long time" after moving in, "medium time" before her Christmas trip to AZ. and "Before they were married" meaning - when he had moved out and was dating Shyanne Pike). Because her sworn testimony definitively eliminated 4th grade, eliminated the start of moving in, and locked the events to the Fall 2018 separation period, the State cannot retroactively salvage a physically impossible claim by asserting the conduct "might have happened" in an unalleged year. Petitioner's newly presented evidence shows calls, videos and photos sent to Jeff Jackson on Christmas 2017 (Ex. J9) which proves 2018 was the only Christmas she went to AZ. With her placing the wedding a year and a half after moving in during the preliminary hearing and further clarifying at trial she remembered late 2018 as "before they got married" definitively removes any possibility she ever intended to convey any other timeframe.

2. The charged conduct describes a specific nighttime, bedtime routine — Petitioner lying in bed in his underwear watching television in the shared family residence while Tiffany Norton was away from the home. This factual scenario requires Petitioner to be living in or cohabitating overnight at the residence. The uncontradicted documentary record proves during the only timeframe in which was ever identified, Petitioner was living elsewhere and did not spend a single night in the home.

This is not a matter of competing testimony or credibility disputes; it is a matter of documented physical impossibility based on the State's own witness testimony and contemporaneous records.

**VI. Contemporaneous Behavioral Evidence Is Incompatible With the Allegation:** The complainant's text and photo evidence begins in December 2017 (when she received a tablet enabling direct messaging) and continues through September 2018, when Petitioner moved out, resuming again after his return. Throughout that period, her mother reported that the complainant "misses u all the time and ask to go over there all the time." (Ex. J12–J13, Oct.–Nov. 2018) The complainant's own direct messages — "I miss you daddy," "Love you," "can you bring cookies by" — reflect ordinary, uncoerced, affectionate contact inconsistent with the State's theory and her own testimony that she feared or avoided him as an abuser. (Ex. J19–J20, J37–J38) This contemporaneous record directly contradicts the complainant's testimony at the protective order hearing that she had "[n]ever" gone fishing with Petitioner and had "[n]ever" told her mother she missed him. (Ex. C11 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 31) At trial, she admitted to a fishing trip occurring while he was moved out — a trip corroborated by contemporaneous photographs and messages dated October 2018. (Ex. J13, J19) Measured against her own trial testimony, the alleged abuse had therefore not occurred as of October 2018, which independently forecloses any theory that the charged conduct began prior to the September 2018 separation or before the June 2018 wedding, further trapping the State in the November–December 2018 window.

The behavioral record shown by not only photos and third party messages but by the complainant's own messages with the petitioner (Ex. J18–J20) beginning on Christmas 2017 and continuing past the alleged conduct, covering the entirety of the twenty one month charging window: a school project completed while smiling, painted nails, skating outings, and — days before a family trip in May 2019 — a photograph of the complainant wearing Petitioner's coveralls, comfortable and happy, pretending to be "dad." (Ex. J14–J15, J21–J26) In forensic credibility evaluations, contemporaneous, unforced affectionate contact and unconstrained shared recreation provide an objective baseline that directly refutes retrospective claims of ongoing fear, avoidance, or terror. *See* *Ralph Underwager & Hollida Wakefield, Accusations of Child Sexual Abuse (1988)*; *Arthur H. Green, True and False Allegations of Sexual Abuse in Child Custody Disputes, 25 J. Am. Acad. Child Psychiatry 449 (1986)*.

**VII. The State's Trial Evidence Was Independently Compromised:** The jury's guilty verdict was produced by a trial record that was structurally compromised by false testimony, material omissions, and prosecutorial misrepresentation.

- No Physical or Forensic Corroboration. No physical, medical, or forensic evidence was introduced at trial.
- The Complainant and Outcry witness were Judicially Impeached. The trial court issued OUJI-CR 9-20 impeachment instructions permitting the jury to disbelieve both the complainant and her father. (Ex. K6–K7)
- False Police Testimony: Detective Judy testified falsely that he could not interview the complainant (Ex. G1 - Trial Tr. III at 36) directly contradicted by the complainant's own protective order hearing testimony confirming she spoke to the "Detective and the police." (Ex. C9 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 27; Ex. C10 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 30)
- Fabricated Res Gestae Timeline: Witness Bailey Ferguson's res gestae testimony rested on an employment timeline that objective documentary records and her own prior statements prove was false (Ex. J27, J29), as detailed in Ground Two, Section A.5.
- Expert Perjury/Contradiction: Christi Cornett denied at trial seeing any signs of coaching, directly contradicting her sworn testimony five months earlier where she admitted the child "clearly had been told or informed as to what to expect." (Ex. L1 - MH 1-21-22 at 33) (Ex. G20 - Trial Tr. III at 119)
- Prosecutorial Misrepresentation: The prosecutor asserted to the jury in closing arguments that "No one ever said 2018" (Ex. H2 - Trial Tr. IV at 96), despite the police report, protective order testimony, and preliminary hearing testimony repeatedly locking the events to late 2018.
- Ineffective / Coerced Representation: Trial counsel possessed every single item mentioned above - all previous testimony, the exculpatory lease, moving photos, travel logs, and text records (Ex. J31–J34) but failed to introduce any of them, and separately coerced Petitioner's waiver of his right to testify. (Ex. I1–I3; Ex. L2–L3)

**VIII. Conclusion:** Considered holistically, as *House*, *Schlup*, and *Fontenot* require:

- The complainant's own account across five proceedings over three years anchored the conduct to a single temporal window November–December 2018.
- The progressive escalation of her account tracked documented, unrecorded meetings with influential adults and the lead prosecutor rather than the natural attributes of memory.
- Objective, uncontested evidence such as: The lease, photographic, assessor, and testimony of the state's witnesses establish that Petitioner was physically absent from the home during that entire window, rendering the alleged bedtime conduct physically impossible. Contemporaneous digital and behavioral records across the entire 21-month charging window show uncoerced affection and baseline comfort incompatible with ongoing abuse.
- The conviction was secured through false testimony, destroyed evidence, pervasive prosecutorial misconduct, judicial abdication, and direct misrepresentation of multiple material facts including the direct false statement by the prosecutor concerning the timeline.

This newly presented evidence is not "merely impeaching"; it is substantive proof of physical impossibility and systematic account contamination. No reasonable juror, presented with this complete record, could find Petitioner guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.

This showing satisfies the *Schlup* gateway, overcoming any procedural default of every claim in this petition. To the extent this Court reaches the question left open in *House v. Bell,* Petitioner submits this showing also independently warrants standalone substantive habeas relief.

<center><h4>GROUND TWO: PETITIONER WAS DENIED HIS SIXTH AMENDMENT RIGHT TO EFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF TRIAL AND APPELLATE COUNSEL UNDER STRICKLAND V. WASHINGTON AND WAS CONSTRUCTIVELY DENIED COUNSEL UNDER UNITED STATES V. CRONIC.</b></center>

Petitioner was deprived of the protections guaranteed under the Sixth Amendment of the United States Constitution. Section A addresses trial counsel under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), and, in the alternative, Number 14 addresses *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984). Section B addresses appellate counsel. As established in the Statement on Deference above, none of these claims was adjudicated on the merits by any state court. This Court reviews all claims de novo.

**SECTION A: INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF TRIAL COUNSEL**

**1. Failure to Present Documentary Alibi Evidence Counsel Had and Never Introduced.** The transcript of the July 29, 2019 protective order hearing, the transcript of the January 15, 2020 preliminary hearing, a lease for a new residence signed September 4, 2018 (Ex. J1), a photograph of a truck and trailer at that residence dated September 8, 2018 (Ex. J3), county assessor records confirming the address (Ex. J2), a text message from that day reading "I'm in the middle of moving" (Ex. J4–J5), messages documenting the complainant's Arizona departure on December 22, 2018 and return January 5, 2019 (Ex. J7–J8), Facebook posts confirming Petitioner's own return to the residence on December 30, 2018 (Ex. J6), text and Facebook message logs from December 2017 through May 2019 showing affectionate, ordinary father-daughter contact (Ex. J12–J26, J37–J38), and contemporaneous photographs of the complainant with Petitioner — fishing in October 2018, painting nails, skating, doing a school project, wearing his coveralls in May 2019 (Ex. J14–J15, J19, J21–J26). State witnesses independently confirmed Petitioner "moved out Summer 18" (Ex. D6 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 65) and "never spent the night" during the separation (Ex. F13 - Trial Tr. II at 87). In opening statement, counsel told the jury it would hear this evidence. He told them: "You'll hear from Shyanne Pike. She'll describe how they went fishing. Brianna asked to go fishing with them." (Ex. F22 - Trial Tr. II at 21). He told them Arizona child welfare records and an Arizona DCS worker would confirm the late-2018 timeframe (Ex. F3 - Trial Tr. II at 23). He told them they would see documents confirming Petitioner's absence during the only window alleged. He then rested without calling a single witness or introducing a single document (Ex. G14 - Trial Tr. III at 165).

Petitioner's emails to defense counsel, produced in post-conviction discovery, confirm he was in possession of this entire body of exculpatory material well before trial. Where the abandoned evidence establishes physical impossibility — the repeated testimony always pinpointing November-December 2018, the sworn testimony of the State's own witness, Tiffany Norton, that Petitioner had moved out and "never spent the night" and the complainant was out of state for most of this window — no strategic account can explain the omission.

Under *Wiggins v. Smith*, 539 U.S. 510, 523 (2003), abandoning available exculpatory evidence without strategic justification is deficient. Under *Conley v. State*, 2014 Ark. 172, ¶ 35, failing to produce evidence promised in opening compounds the harm, since jurors are left to infer the promised proof never existed — "[l]ittle is more damaging than to fail to produce important evidence that had been promised in an opening." *Anderson v. Butler*, 858 F.2d 16, 17 (1st Cir. 1988).

The prejudice is not abstract. The jury heard the complainant's trial testimony standing alone, devoid of the context the prior transcripts would have provided: more than fourteen times across three years of proceedings she anchored the events to December 2018; the evolution of the account tracked in Section IV of Ground One; Tiffany Norton's own testimony confirming Petitioner's absence. Had the jury seen the protective order hearing transcript and the preliminary hearing transcript — each independently corroborating the late-2018 timeline, each independently documenting the State's own witness confirming Petitioner's physical absence — no rational juror could have credited the State's theory that the conduct occurred at some unspecified time within a twenty-one-month window. The omission of the lease, the moving photographs, the text messages, the Arizona travel records, and the contemporaneous behavioral evidence was separately and independently prejudicial: each piece of evidence, standing alone, was inconsistent with guilt; taken together, they proved impossibility. The jury saw none of it.

**2. Failure to Subpoena and Call Promised Material Witnesses.** Counsel told the jury in opening: "You'll hear from Shyanne Pike. She'll describe how they went fishing. Brianna asked to go fishing with them. She took them to a farm that her family owns and did things with her out there." (Ex. F22 - Trial Tr. II at 21). He listed Arizona DCS worker Heather Slater as a witness who would corroborate the complainant's own Arizona statements (Ex. K10). He told the jury the Arizona records would confirm "November-December" as the timeframe (Ex. F3 - Trial Tr. II at 23). Slater was never subpoenaed; neither witness was called; the defense rested with no witnesses at all (Ex. G14 - Trial Tr. III at 165).

Under *Anderson*, 858 F.2d at 17, and *Felts v. State*, 354 S.W.3d 266, 277–78 (Tenn. 2011), abandoning a promise for reasons apparent when the promise was made is not strategy. Pike's testimony — that the complainant voluntarily sought out time with Petitioner during the separation, asked to go fishing, and spent time with her at her family's farm — would have directly undercut the State's narrative that the complainant feared Petitioner and avoided him. The Arizona records would have shown the complainant initially denied the second charge and contradicted the State's narrative on multiple points. The prejudice from their absence is not that the jury heard nothing from the defense — it is that the jury was told it would hear from these witnesses, was primed to evaluate their testimony, and was then left to infer, from their absence, that the promised testimony would have been adverse. *Conley*, 2014 Ark. 172, ¶ 35.

Had counsel confronted Judy with the transcript, the jury would have learned that the lead detective's central claim — the claim that explained away every evidentiary gap in the State's case — was false. A witness whose own sworn accounts of when her employment ended cannot agree with each other — and whose employment, on the documentary record, in fact ended roughly a year before either account — Ferguson's testimony was extremely prejudicial and inflammatory allowing it to stand while possessing undeniable proof that it was entirely false is objectively unreasonable and presents exactly the foundational weakness *Strickland* requires counsel to test.

The prejudice from this failure is direct and structural. Ferguson's testimony was the most inflammatory in the case: she alleged Petitioner sent her unsolicited photographs of his penis and made inappropriate comments about her body. The jury received no limiting instruction on this testimony (See Ground Three). Its admissibility as res gestae was improper upon itself as it was not "so closely connected to the charged offense as to form part of the entire transaction; b) it is necessary to give the jury a complete understanding of the crime; or c) when it is central to the chain of events." Id. *See also Tafolla*, 2019 OK CR 15, ¶ 16, 446 P.3d at 1257. — Ferguson's testimony was entirely unrelated to the charged acts in any way; it simply provided a reason for Tiffany to have her Ex. Husband make the inquiry — her own testimony contradicted itself on multiple points and the objective time and date stamped messages prove it was false. Had counsel impeached her with the documents he possessed, the foundation for her testimony would have crumbled on cross-examination, before the jury. At minimum, the jury would have been presented with a witness whose central timeline claim was demonstrably false by a full year. At maximum, the prejudicial testimony would have been excluded. Either outcome was available; counsel achieved neither.

**6. This was not a minor inconsistency. The forensic interview was the centerpiece of the State's case — the only recorded statement of the complainant, played for the jury, around which every other piece of evidence was organized. Its own examiner — the State's own expert witness — had acknowledged under oath, five months before trial, that the complainant appeared to have been prepped, to have known what the interview was for and what to expect. Counsel allowed her to testify at trial to the exact opposite without confrontation.

The prejudice is that the jury heard the forensic interview presented as the product of a neutral, uncontaminated process, when the State's own expert had already acknowledged signs of contamination — the single fact most likely to undermine the reliability the jury accorded it. The interview was played. The jury believed it. Counsel had the transcript that would have told the jury it was not what it appeared to be. He never used it.

**7. Failure to Object to the Admission of an Unauthenticated Substitute Forensic Interview Disk.** At the January 21, 2022 hearing on the reliability of the forensic interview, Christi Cornett authenticated the specific recording the court would later rely on:

- Q. And how do you know that this disk itself, State's Exhibit No. 1, is that recording?
  - A. Because I observed it earlier today and initialed and dated when I saw it. (Ex. L4 - MH 1-21-22 at 10)

On the morning of June 23, 2022 — the third day of trial, roughly five months after the January hearing — the court announced it had reviewed that disk the previous night:

- THE COURT: Last night, I watched the forensic interview dated July 22, 2019, conducted at the Mary Abbott House by Ms. Cornett of Brianna Jackson. I have the — my disk is labeled State's Exhibit 1. I'm not sure if that — I think that was from the preliminary hearing or something.
- MR. NEDWICK: Motion hearing.
- MS. AUSTIN: The motion hearing.
- THE COURT: Yeah, the motion hearing. So I don't want to confuse the record. Sorry about that. But anyways, I got the disk. I watched it last night… I believe that the interview was conducted in such a way that comports with the statutory requirements that it would be admissible and be able to be shown to the jury. (Ex. G7 - Trial Tr. III at 5)

Immediately following this ruling, the prosecutor stated:

- MS. AUSTIN: Okay. And I think we could probably go ahead and mark that Court's Exhibit 1, so I'll get a different sticker for that, because we have agreed on some redactions to the forensic interview… We have shown them the redacted copy. They've agreed to it. That's the one that we intend upon admitting through Ms. Cornett. So for record purposes, that would be great to have that as Court's 1.
- THE COURT: Very good. If you'll take care of that, I'll give you the DVD. (Ex. G7 - Trial Tr. III at 5)

The record does not reflect that Petitioner personally was addressed regarding, or personally agreed to, the redaction described; the only agreement placed on the record was counsel's.

No relabeled "Court's Exhibit 1" bears Cornett's initials or was ever offered to the jury. Instead, when Cornett's testimony resumed before the jury:

- Q: And it says on there, Redacted. You've had a chance to watch it. Is there a snippet of something that was taken out because the Court has told us to take something out?
  - A: Yes. There's like 12 seconds.
- Q: Okay. And so other than that, is this accurate as to the interview you conducted with B.J.?
  - A: Yes.
- MS. AUSTIN: Your Honor, at this time the State moves for admission of State's Exhibit No. 6.
- MR. NEDWICK: Same objections, your Honor, and plus repetitious at this point.
- THE COURT: Objection will be overruled. State's Exhibit 6 will be admitted into evidence. You may publish. (Ex. G2 - Trial Tr. III at 122)

State's Exhibit 6 — an admittedly altered, redacted version that was not reviewed by the Court prior to admission. Not the disk the court reviewed and ruled on — State's Exhibit 1 / Court's Exhibit 1 which Cornett had authenticated by her own initials and the date she wrote on it — was played for the jury. Cornett's authentication testimony was never extended to Exhibit 6 by exhibit number. The court conducted no further reliability review of whatever redacted version Exhibit 6 in fact was. Counsel's "Same objections… plus repetitious" did not identify, and did not preserve, the distinct problem that the exhibit being offered was not the exhibit the court had reviewed and ruled on.

Under *Strickland*, an objection that fails to identify the specific defect in the specific exhibit being offered leaves that defect unpreserved. And under *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984), and *Sisk v. State*, 192 A.2d 108, 110 (Md. 1963), the admission of an unauthenticated substitute for the centerpiece of the prosecution's case is not a technical oversight but a foundational failure. The prejudice is not that counsel made the wrong objection — it is that he made an objection that, on its face, failed to alert the court to the actual problem: the jury was about to see a disk the court had never reviewed, never authenticated, and never ruled admissible. The disk the court reviewed was State's Exhibit 1, bearing Cornett's initials. The disk the jury saw was State's Exhibit 6, bearing a redaction the court never reviewed. They may not have been the same. The integrity of the most important piece of evidence in the case was compromised, and counsel's objection — the only mechanism available to prevent the compromise — failed to address it.

**8. Failure to Seek Any Remedy for the State's "Bad-Faith" Destruction of Exculpatory Body-Worn Camera Evidence.** Officer Pierce's BWC recording captured the original, more limited disclosure: an incident confined to the living room couch, no completed second act ("Did NOT touch Dallas penis"), and the December 2018 timeframe (Ex. A2–A3). That recording — the single most important piece of exculpatory evidence in the case, capturing the account before any coaching, before any forensic interview, before the story evolved — was destroyed. A second recording from the same night, from the same Officer Pierce of Tiffany Norton, was preserved (Ex. A5, F17 - Trial Tr. II at 116). Counsel sought no mistrial, no adverse-inference instruction, and no curative measure of any kind.

The significance of this recording cannot be overstated. It would have preserved an audio recording of the original account that: (1) placed the incident on the living room couch, not the bedroom as later alleged; (2) explicitly denied the second count — "Did NOT touch Dallas penis"; and (3) confined the incident to November–December 2018, during which Petitioner was physically absent from the home. Every deviation from that original account — the location change, the escalation of the conduct, the addition of the second count — occurred after the recording was destroyed. The recording would have frozen the account at the moment of first disclosure, before the five documented conversations in the first week, before the forensic interview, before the meeting with the prosecutor. Its destruction deprived Petitioner of contemporaneous, objective recorded proof of what was actually said on the night the allegations first surfaced. Its destruction allowed the state to argue, as it did in closing, that Officer Pierce wrote it down wrong.

Under *Arizona v. Youngblood*, 488 U.S. 51, 58 (1988), destruction of materially exculpatory evidence — particularly where, as here, contemporaneous non-exculpatory evidence from the identical circumstances was preserved — supports an inference of bad faith and entitles the defense to a remedy. An adverse inference instruction, at minimum, would have permitted the jury to conclude the destroyed recording was unfavorable to the State. Counsel sought nothing.

The prejudice is that the petitioner was deprived of the ability to demonstrate the definitive timing placement and absolute denial of the second act in a manner equivalent to the state's repeated use of the interview video. The recording would also have completely destroyed Jeff Jackson's testimony which was completely different in every regard. It allowed the prosecutor to argue in closing: "i want you to get it accurate every time. And then I want you to make sure that that police officer wrote it down 100 percent right. Think about that. Just think about that for a second." Suggesting that Officer Pierce just wrote it down wrong dismissed the paper report entirely.

**9. Counsel Admitted On Record He Failed To Prepare.** Counsel told the court on the morning of closing: "about 1:00 this morning, I realized that. And so anyway, best I could, I tried to recall the witnesses that I knew had inconsistent statements." (Ex. G12 - Trial Tr. IV at 5). He initially proposed an impeachment instruction for Bailey Ferguson, then withdrew it because he was "not sure that was clearly inconsistent" and it "could have been a misunderstanding." (Ex. G15 - Trial Tr. IV at 7–8)

Ferguson's testimony was, as Section 5 above demonstrates, independently documented as false — not a matter of memory or nuance but a matter of comparing her two sworn accounts against each other and against Facebook messages in counsel's own file. Further, he had impeached her with her own testimony concerning the photos and conversation, and solidified through cross examining Det. Judy about the same issue in her interview with him. It was indeed clearly inconsistent. Counsel's on-record admission that he began thinking about impeachment instructions at 1:00 a.m. on the morning of closing arguments, and then withdrew one because he could not remember the testimony correctly, is itself an admission of deficient performance.

The prejudice is that the court had already issued impeachment instructions for the complainant and Jeffrey Jackson (Ex. K6–K7). The jury was formally told it could find those two witnesses not believable. Ferguson — whose testimony was the most inflammatory, whose timeline was demonstrably false, and who was the only witness whose testimony was admitted as res gestae — received no such instruction. No limiting instruction, nothing. Due to the admitted last minute preparation he failed to recall the other inconsistent statements shown previously, Six of the State's Seven witnesses could have and should have been impeached. The jury was left to consider their inconsistent, demonstrably false, testimony without any guidance that they too, had previously testified differently and could be found not credible. Under *United States v. Leonard*, 494 F.2d 955, 963 (D.C. Cir. 1974), impeachment evidence without a cautioning instruction leaves the jury without the framework to use it properly. Here, the jury received no framework at all for the witnesses whose framework was most needed. Had counsel presented the previous testimony detailed above, six out of seven state witnesses would have been impeached, there's a strong likelihood that no juror could convict beyond a reasonable doubt if they had been informed by jury instructions that every state witness had previously testified differently.

**10. Failure to Object to Pervasive Prosecutorial Misconduct Throughout Voir Dire and Closing.** As catalogued at length in Ground Three, Section B, below — using a potato soup analogy (Ex. E2 - Trial Tr. I at 119) to describe the elements of a crime and the high burden of beyond a reasonable doubt, the false representation that no reasonable-doubt instruction would issue (Ex. E6 - Trial Tr. I at 186) having a juror publicly describe her first sexual encounter (Ex. E1 - Trial Tr. I at 138) over 185 conditioning questions in voir dire, then repeated vouching for two judicially impeached witnesses (Ex. H4 - Trial Tr. IV at 78) the knowing misrepresentation of the timeline (Ex. H2 - Trial Tr. IV at 96) golden-rule arguments, inflammatory epithets, burden-shifting, and improper "burden-met" declarations, violating the presumption of innocence by repeatedly declaring petitioners guilt using statements such as the petitioner "is guilty beyond a reasonable doubt" or "You have to be punished for doing this to a child, because you know what he did." — counsel objected to none of it.

The prejudice from this silence is not measured instance by instance; it is measured by what the jury heard, uncorrected, at the two points in trial where an objection matters most. During voir dire, the jury was conditioned — through 185 leading questions asked before any evidence was introduced — to accept the complainant's word alone, to excuse the absence of physical evidence, and to apply a standard of proof trivialized to a soup recipe. During closing, the jury was told — without objection — that the State had "met its burden" on every element, that "we know these things occurred" "is not inconsistent" "she had no reason to lie" the complainant was "telling the truth," that her father was "not lying," that "no one ever said 2018," and that Petitioner was a "child molester" and "sex offender" and "guilty beyond a reasonable doubt." None of this was met with an objection. None of it was corrected. The jury deliberated on a record shaped entirely by the State's unchecked presentation.

Under the reasoning applied in *Melvin Goodspeed v. State*, No. 2-01-073-CR, 2003 WL 22271258, at *5 (Tex. App. Oct. 2, 2003), silence in the face of this volume and character of misconduct is not restraint; it is abandonment of the adversarial function. The jury heard only one side of the case — the State's — because counsel never forced the court to regulate the other, coerced his client and presented no defense at all.

**11. Failure to Strike a Juror With Direct, Traumatic Exposure to the Identical Offense.** Juror M.K. disclosed during voir dire that her brother had been convicted of lewd acts with a child and that her daughter's best friend, a victim of the same offense, had died by suicide because of it. Counsel did not use a peremptory strike, and M.K. sat on the jury that convicted Petitioner of the identical offense.

No strategic justification can account for this. A juror whose sibling was convicted of lewd acts and whose child's best friend committed suicide as a result of the same crime cannot, no matter what they claim, evaluate evidence of lewd acts impartially. Her presence on the jury violated *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961), which guarantees an impartial jury. Under *People v. Manning*, 948 N.E.2d 542, 566–67 (Ill. 2011) (Freeman, J., dissenting), retaining a juror with this degree of direct personal exposure to the exact charge cannot be squared with any conceivable strategy. The prejudice is structural: a biased juror deliberated on guilt, and this Court cannot know — and need not try to determine — how her presence affected the verdict. The Constitution does not require such speculation; it requires an impartial jury, and Petitioner did not receive one.

**12. Coerced Waiver of the Right to Testify.** During the lunch recess on the final day of testimony, counsel told Petitioner the defense would call no witnesses and he "did not need to testify." Petitioner's contemporaneous letter to the court states the waiver was "not of my choosing" and that he was told to "just agree with what was asked." (Ex. I1–I3). This was the first Petitioner heard of the decision to abandon the defense entirely — a defense counsel had promised the jury in opening, for which witnesses had been listed and subpoenas prepared, and which Petitioner had been preparing, for over 3 years denying every plea offer adamant to prove his innocence and which he had been led to believe would be presented.

The colloquy itself was conducted immediately after the break in a rushed and whispered bench conference with the jury seated in the courtroom. Co-counsel Charles Douglas — from the same office as the attorney who had just made the decision for him — asked every question, all in leading form: "Have you felt any pressure from Mr. Nedwick or myself in respect to either preparing the defense or calling witnesses?" and "And has Mr. Nedwick or myself at all ever pressured you into making the decision not to call witnesses?" and "Do you make this freely of your own free will and volition?" (Ex. L2–L6). The court asked nothing — not whether Petitioner understood his rights, not whether he had been pressured, not why a defense planned for years was being abandoned mid-trial only minutes before the state would rest. The court interjected only to reposition Petitioner after the prosecutor complained it was "getting a little loud." (Ex. L2). At the conclusion, the court stated only: "Okay. I have no further questions. Thank you." (Ex. L6)

Under *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 52 (1987), the right to testify is personal and fundamental. Waiver must be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). A colloquy in which the judge asks nothing, all questioning comes from the attorney accused of coercion, and the defendant has documented — in a contemporaneous letter which aligns perfectly with the record — that the waiver was "not of my choosing," and was told to just agree and give "yes sir" type answers is not a valid waiver. It is a nullity.

The prejudice is structural and immense. Petitioner was denied the opportunity to tell the jury, in his own words: that he did not live in the home during the only window ever alleged; that the complainant was in Arizona for a portion of that window; that the messages the jury never saw showed ordinary, affectionate contact; that the photographs showed the complainant happy and comfortable with him through May 2019; that the State's case rested on a timeline the documentary record disproved. The jury heard only the State's narrative. The one person who could have told them otherwise was silenced — not by his own choice, but because his lawyer told him, during lunch only a few hours before he was expecting to begin the defense on the third day of a trial where he is facing the possibility of two life sentences and having sat through days of being called names and made out to be a monster that he "did not need to testify" — it "can only make it worse" "they will twist your words" "your case cannot get any better" — despite all of this the petitioner argued until he was out of time, outmatched by a professional litigator, he surrendered feeling he had no choice the lawyer was refusing.

**13. Actual Conflict of Interest — Counsel Attacked His Own Client's Credibility at Sentencing.** At the combined sentencing and new-trial hearing, counsel told the court he would not argue paragraphs 8 through 12 of the new-trial motion — paragraphs that alleged his own misconduct in coercing the waiver: "The Court will notice that each of those sentences begin with, Defendant alleges. Those are not assertions that I necessarily agree with, which leaves me in a difficult position." (Ex. I10 - Sent. Tr. at 8). Counsel told the court that if Petitioner "wants to be heard on those and provide testimony," counsel had "no objection to him being heard on those," but reiterated: "those are not things I can argue, because I don't — I don't agree with them." (Ex. I10 - Sent. Tr. at 8)

The court placed Petitioner under oath. Petitioner began to describe what had happened at the lunch recess: "That was not of my choosing, not even a thought process of mine, you know, that we subpoenaed multiple witnesses to testify and had plans for a defense. At the close — we broke for lunch one day, I believe it was the day that the State rested. And on the way out of the courthouse, Mr. Nedwick said that we were not going to call any witnesses that day, and I did not need to testify." (Ex. I12 - Sent. Tr. at 9)

Counsel immediately interrupted: "Your Honor, I have to as my duty, for candor of the Court, I can't allow him to testify to something I know is false. I didn't say those words ever. But to say that I even had a conversation about that as we're leaving the courthouse is absolutely false." (Ex. I8 - Sent. Tr. at 10). The court responded only, "Thank you. That will be noted. Go ahead, Mr. Norton." Petitioner continued: "In my memory, that is what was said. We were not going to call any witnesses…" (Ex. I13 - Sent. Tr. at 10)

Later in the same hearing, after Petitioner described the whispered colloquy and stated he had been instructed to answer "yes, sir" to whatever counsel asked, counsel again spoke against him: "I'm sure you don't want to hear all the contradictions, but just as — just to make sure I do my duty and candor to the Court, much of what he just testified to is not true." Petitioner responded, "In his opinion," and counsel replied, "Not in my opinion. Factually." (Ex. I14 - Sent. Tr. at 11). The court told counsel he had, "as is any licensed member of the bar, an ethical obligation to be truthful," and turned to Petitioner: "I've got to determine who's being truthful or not. So is there anything else you want to tell me? I understand that there was an affidavit, I believe, sent by your father. Do you want to address that?" Petitioner answered that he had not heard the underlying conversation firsthand and had "nothing… to address on that."

The sequence — refuse to argue, falsely claim no objection, invite testimony, then ambush — was not an accident. Counsel knew in advance that paragraphs 8 through 12 alleged his own misconduct. He had time to arrange for co-counsel Charles Douglas to argue those paragraphs. He had time to move to withdraw. He did neither. Instead, he told the court he had "no objection" to Petitioner speaking — a representation that was false, because the moment Petitioner spoke, counsel objected, interrupted, and branded him a liar. The ambush was premeditated.

Under *Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 446 U.S. 335, 348–50 (1980), an actual conflict of interest presumes prejudice without further inquiry. Under *Cargle v. Mullin*, 317 F.3d 1196, 1202 (10th Cir. 2003), counsel who exhibits "actual doubt and hostility toward his client's case" commits "a fundamental violation of his duty of loyalty." Here counsel did not merely doubt his client; at the one proceeding where Petitioner's own account of the coerced waiver could have been credited — the sentencing and new-trial hearing — counsel testified against him. He told the court, in Petitioner's presence, that Petitioner's sworn account was "false," "factually" — and the court, rather than inquiring further, credited counsel's ethical obligation over Petitioner's testimony. The prejudice is not merely that the new-trial motion was denied; it is that the very attorney charged with advocating for a new trial aligned himself with the prosecution on the most critical factual dispute in the case — whether Petitioner had freely chosen to remain silent or had been silenced by his own lawyer.

**14. Cumulative Effect — Constructive Denial of Counsel Under Cronic.** Taken together: counsel abandoned a documented, physically dispositive alibi defense he had promised the jury, including the protective order hearing transcript, the preliminary hearing transcript, a lease, moving records, text messages, travel records, Facebook message logs, and photographs; never called a witness or introduced an exhibit; left the State's witnesses' demonstrable falsehoods and internal contradictions entirely unimpeached — multiple contradictions and falsehoods from Tiffany alone, Bailey Ferguson invented a whole year, Detective Judy's provably false claim that he could not interview the complainant, and Christi Cornett's contradictory testimony on coaching — despite possessing the documents and transcripts to prove each one; failed to object to a foundational chain-of-custody break in the State's central exhibit, allowing the jury to view a disk the court had never reviewed; sought no remedy for the destruction of the most exculpatory evidence in the case; prepared his only two impeachment instructions from memory at 1:00 a.m. and then withdrew it; sat silent through well over 250 instances of prosecutorial misconduct in voir dire and closing; seated a juror with direct personal exposure to the identical offense; coerced his client's waiver of the right to testify and participated in a colloquy conducted entirely by interested counsel with no judicial inquiry; and then intentionally engineered an attack on his own client by refusing to argue, falsely claiming no objections, then took the stand, in effect, to attack his own client's credibility at sentencing, telling the court his client's sworn account was "factually" false only to protect his own interests.

Under *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984), where counsel "entirely fails to subject the prosecution's case to meaningful adversarial testing," prejudice is presumed without the ordinary *Strickland* inquiry into individual errors. Even were this Court to conclude no single ground above independently warrants relief, their aggregate — spanning pretrial through sentencing, touching every witness the State called, and culminating in counsel affirmatively undermining his own client's testimony — establishes exactly the systemic breakdown *Cronic* and *Cargle v. Mullin*, 317 F.3d 1196, 1206 (10th Cir. 2003), were written to address. A defendant whose counsel cannot function effectively "is in no better position than one who has no counsel at all." *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 396 (1985). Prejudice is presumed. The conviction cannot stand.

**SECTION B: INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF APPELLATE COUNSEL**

**1. Constructive Denial of Appellate Counsel Under Cronic.** Three appointed attorneys handled Petitioner's direct appeal in succession, and none functioned as an advocate. Ricki Walterscheid, appointed September 2022, performed no work and never communicated with Petitioner (Ex. M1–M2). The case was reassigned to Virginia Sanders on January 5, 2023 (Ex. M3). In a sworn affidavit dated March 23, 2023 — one day before the brief was due under a prior extension — Sanders stated she had only "just started writing" and "still need[ed] to research some of the issues." (Ex. M8–M9). She filed the Brief of Appellant less than two weeks later, on April 7, 2023, without ever meeting Petitioner, speaking to him or allowing him to review it. Before filing it, she wrote repeatedly on every communication to actively discourage the appeal itself: "If you were given a new trial, you could receive two, consecutive life sentences… I want to emphasize what you are risking by appealing this case." (Ex. M6). When Petitioner provided her with case law he believed applicable, she responded, "After more than 30 years of practicing criminal law, I am fully equipped to know what law and cases apply to your appeal" (Ex. M7) — an acknowledgment that she was aware of the material and made a conscious decision not to use it. She had repeatedly represented that she and an investigator would visit Petitioner in person with her; that visit never occurred (Ex. M6). She retired from practice shortly after filing. Wendi Hobbs was then appointed though not officially; she also refused communications with petitioner outside of formal updates, until after the appeal had been affirmed she did not respond until the rehearing window had passed, refused to raise any of the omitted meritorious issues, and admitted she knew "nothing of the case" and it was only given to her to "see it through the process." (Ex. M16)

Under *Cronic*, 466 U.S. at 659, when counsel "entirely fails to subject the prosecution's case to meaningful adversarial testing," prejudice is presumed. A brief drafted from a standing start in roughly two weeks, filed by a lawyer who was simultaneously warning her own client away from the very appeal she was appointed to prosecute, dismissive of case law he provided her, and never even had a phone conversation before filing, is not substandard performance to be measured against a but-for-prejudice yardstick — it is the absence of an advocate, the precise circumstance *Cronic* addresses. *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 396. The successor attorney's refusal even to seek rehearing on omitted issues compounded rather than cured the abandonment.

The OCCA claimed to evaluate this claim solely under *Strickland*, declining to apply *Cronic* at all — an unreasonable application of clearly established federal law, since a total constructive denial of counsel is exactly the category of case *Cronic* carved out from ordinary prejudice analysis. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1). But regardless of the OCCA's treatment, this claim, like all claims in this petition, was never adjudicated on the merits by a state court that actually possessed the evidence, as established in the Statement on Deference above. This Court reviews it de novo. Prejudice is presumed.

**2. Appellate Counsel Omitted Clearly Stronger Claims in Favor of Weak Ones.** Even if this Court declines to find total constructive denial, Sanders's performance independently fails *Strickland*. Appellate counsel need not raise every non-frivolous claim, *Smith v. Robbins*, 528 U.S. 259, 288 (2000), but the presumption of effectiveness gives way "when [the] ignored issues are clearly stronger than those presented," *id.* (quoting *Gray v. Greer*, 800 F.2d 644, 646 (7th Cir. 1986)), and in the Tenth Circuit, an omitted issue "so plainly meritorious that it would have been unreasonable to winnow it out" can establish deficiency on its own. *Cargle v. Mullin*, 317 F.3d 1196, 1202 (10th Cir. 2003).

Sanders raised six propositions, each narrowly and thinly argued:

1. Generic sufficiency of the evidence with no mention of impeachment;
2. Ferguson's res gestae testimony without any reference to her contradicted timeline;
3. The failure to strike Juror M.K.;
4. Prosecutorial misconduct limited to roughly fifteen instances out of the more than 250 documented in the appendix;
5. Ineffective assistance of trial counsel confined to the broken opening-statement promise;
6. Cumulative error.

Left out entirely were the following clearly stronger claims, each apparent from the trial record, each requiring no further factual development to present:

(1) Bad-faith destruction of exculpatory BWC evidence. The original, contemporaneous recording of the initial disclosure — documenting the couch location, the explicit denial of the second count, and the December 2018 timeframe — was destroyed while non-exculpatory evidence from the same officer, same night, was preserved. This was a textbook *Youngblood*/*Brady* violation.

(2) Multiple *Napue* violations. Detective Judy's false "can't talk to her" testimony, contradicted by the complainant's own protective order hearing testimony. Tiffany Norton's nine independent false and inconsistent statements across four proceedings. Bailey Ferguson's fabricated employment timeline, contradicted by Facebook messages in the record. Christi Cornett's contradictory testimony on coaching — acknowledging at the pretrial hearing that the complainant "clearly had been told or informed as to what to expect," then denying any sign of coaching at trial. Each was a *Napue* violation. Counsel raised none.

(3) Coerced waiver of the right to testify. A structural error under *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. at 52, apparent from the trial transcript and Petitioner's contemporaneous letter. Appellate counsel raised no claim.

(4) Trial counsel's actual conflict of interest and public attack on his client at sentencing. Apparent from the sentencing transcript, warranting presumed prejudice under *Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 446 U.S. at 348–50, and constituting a "fundamental violation of his duty of loyalty" under *Cargle*, 317 F.3d at 1202. Appellate counsel raised no claim.

(5) Prosecutorial vouching for judicially impeached witnesses. Sanders raised a limited vouching claim but omitted the critical fact that the vouched-for witnesses — the complainant and Jeffrey Jackson — had been judicially impeached by OUJI-CR 9-20 instructions. The prosecutor vouched for them at least twenty-four times in closing. The omission of this single fact transformed a weak generic-bolstering claim into a dead-bang winner. Appellate counsel raised neither the fact nor the claim.

(6) The prosecutor's knowing false statement. "No one ever said 2018" was demonstrably false on the existing record. This was a *Napue*/*Miller v. Pate* violation requiring reversal. Appellate counsel raised no claim.

(7) Admission of an unauthenticated substitute forensic interview disk. The jury viewed State's Exhibit 6; the court reviewed State's Exhibit 1. The disks were physically distinct and the substitute was never authenticated. Appellate counsel raised no claim.

(8) Erroneous sufficiency standard. The trial court and OCCA failed to apply the controlling Oklahoma corroboration requirement under *Jones v. State*, 765 P.2d 800 (Okla. Crim. App. 1988), and *Salyer v. State*, 761 P.2d 890 (Okla. Crim. App. 1988), which require corroboration where a child's testimony is improbable or impeached. Here the complainant was judicially impeached. Appellate counsel raised no claim.

(9) Full scope of prosecutorial misconduct. Sanders raised approximately 15 instances out of 265 documented — fewer than 6%. Over 94% of the misconduct — including the potato soup analogy, the golden rule hypothetical asking a female juror to describe her last consensual sexual encounter, the burden-shifting "complain to the chief" argument, the inflammatory epithets, and the systematic burden-met declarations — was never presented to the OCCA.

Each of these omitted claims was record-based, legally well-established, and required no further factual development to present — precisely the category *Cargle* says cannot reasonably be winnowed out even from a strong appeal, let alone from the six underdeveloped propositions Sanders chose instead. That she prepared her brief in roughly two weeks from a standing start, never consulted her client, dismissed the case law he provided her, and was simultaneously discouraging him from appealing at all is not the profile of an attorney making considered strategic choices among competing strong claims; it is the profile of an attorney who did not know the record well enough to recognize which claims were strong. The omission of claims this clearly stronger, standing alongside the abandonment documented in Section B.1, deprived Petitioner of the appeal the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee.

The prejudice from the omission of these claims is established by what they would have shown the OCCA. Had the BWC destruction claim been raised, the OCCA would have confronted the State's selective preservation of evidence — a due process violation of the kind that routinely results in reversal. Had the *Napue* claims been raised, the OCCA would have seen that the State's case rested on false testimony from four separate witnesses. Had the coerced-waiver claim been raised — a structural error under *Rock* — reversal would have been automatic. Had the vouching-for-impeached-witnesses claim been raised with the critical fact of judicial impeachment, the OCCA would have seen that the prosecutor told the jury to believe witnesses the court had formally instructed it to view with caution. Any one of these claims, properly presented, created a reasonable probability — indeed, a likelihood — of a different outcome on appeal. The omission of all ten, while raising weak and underdeveloped alternatives, destroyed any chance of meaningful appellate review. The conviction must be vacated.

(Ex. A2–A3) Pierce confirmed the camera was activated and the recording properly uploaded and stored. (Ex. F17 - Trial Tr. II at 116) Detective Judy testified he never reviewed it and that it had been "purged." (Ex. G13 - Trial Tr. III at 68) Within the same hour, Pierce recorded an interview with Tiffany Norton that contained nothing exculpatory — that recording was preserved.

Selective preservation of the non-exculpatory recording alongside destruction of the exculpatory one, from the same officer on the same night, supports the inference of bad faith that *Arizona v. Youngblood*, 488 U.S. 51, 58 (1988), requires. Where identity or the physical mechanics of the offense are at issue and the State permits destruction of evidence "that could eliminate a defendant as the perpetrator," due process is violated and dismissal is the remedy "unless the evidence against the defendant is so strong that a court can say beyond a reasonable doubt that the destroyed evidence would not have proved exonerating." *State v. Youngblood*, 153 Ariz. 50, 52 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1986). The State cannot make that showing here: the destroyed recording would have provided an audio recording of the original account that placed the offense within a window the documentary record independently proves Petitioner was physically absent for. The state may argue the paper police report still preserved that information for the jury, but they did not use a paper copy of the forensic interview. The state's use of a video, then replaying it again at closing demonstrates that the state understands the impact of seeing and hearing information over a simple paper copy. Independently, even if bad faith is not inferred, its suppression whether by the state or by Norman Police still violates *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963).

The recording was the single most important piece of exculpatory evidence: it captured the account before five documented adult conversations in the first week, before the forensic interview, before the meeting with the prosecutor, before every escalation documented in Ground One, Section IV. Its destruction deprived Petitioner of the most powerful contemporaneous record of what was actually said. The prejudice is that the jury had to settle for a paper copy, which the state in closing dismissed as Officer Pierce may have written it down wrong, a tactic they would never employ for a police report that helped their case.

**2. Knowing Use of Detective Judy's False Testimony:** Judy testified he "can't talk to" the complainant. (Ex. G1 - Trial Tr. III at 36) The complainant's own sworn protective order hearing testimony establishes the opposite: she "talked to Detectives" and "gave statements," correcting defense counsel when he omitted the word "Detective." (Ex. C9 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 27; Ex. C10 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 30) The State's own witness list confirmed DHS "conducted interviews along with law enforcement in this case." (Ex. K9) The State possessed the transcript and never corrected Judy's testimony. Under *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264, 269 (1959), the prohibition on knowing false testimony "does not cease to apply merely because the false testimony goes only to the credibility of the witness," and under *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150, 154 (1972), the duty to correct runs to the entire prosecution's office. The materiality bar is a "reasonable likelihood" of affecting the jury's judgment, *United States v. Brawer*, 496 F.2d 703, 707 (2d Cir. 1974). A lead investigator's false claim about the basic scope of his own investigation — offered as the explanation for the total absence of corroboration — easily clears it.

**3. Knowing Use of Tiffany Norton's False and Inconsistent Testimony:** Norton's testimony conflicts with her own prior statements on at least nine material points across the police report, protective order hearing, preliminary hearing, and trial, as detailed in Ground Two, Section A.4, above: the reconciliation timeline, the moved-back-in date, the divorce papers, the circumstances of learning the allegation, Petitioner's response to the confrontation, the complainant's age, Petitioner's employment, Ferguson's age, and Norton's familiarity with the State's photographic evidence. The State possessed the police report and the prior hearing transcripts containing each of these contradictions and corrected none of them at trial. *United States v. Meinster*, 619 F.2d 1041, 1051 (4th Cir. 1980) (Sobeloff, J., dissenting), describes the *Napue* materiality bar as a deliberately "low threshold" precisely because false testimony "corrupt\[s\] the truth-seeking function of the trial process" regardless of subject matter. Nine independent, document-and-transcript-contradicted statements from a single corroborating witness, left uncorrected, meet that threshold many times over.

**4. Knowing Use of Bailey Ferguson's False and Internally Inconsistent Employment Timeline:** Ferguson gave two different, mutually inconsistent sworn accounts of when her employment ended — "October of 2018" at the January 21, 2022 hearing, and "spring, early summer… 2018" at trial — and both accounts are independently contradicted by Facebook messages showing Petitioner's father, not Ferguson, handling childcare responsibilities by October 2017 and January 2018. (Ex. J16, J29, J36) Ferguson's account of whether the photographs were preceded by same-day communication also shifted between the hearing ("Not that I recall") and trial ("we were having conversation"). The State never corrected the record. Her fabricated and self-contradictory timeline was the sole foundation for admitting her testimony as temporally connected to the charged conduct; without it, the testimony was too remote to qualify as res gestae. *United States v. Quinn*, 537 F. Supp. 2d 99, 117 (D.D.C. 2008).

**SECTION B: PERVASIVE PROSECUTORIAL MISCONDUCT DEPRIVED PETITIONER OF A FAIR TRIAL**

**1. Knowing False Statement Concealing Timeline Impossibility:** The prosecutor's closing statement — "No one ever said 2018" (Ex. H2 - Trial Tr. IV at 96) — was not advocacy but a misrepresentation of a record she had personally examined. The police report anchored the offense window to November 29–December 25, 2018. (Ex. A1–A2) The protective order hearing testimony repeatedly placed events "before last Christmas" (Ex. C3 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 13; Ex. C5 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 16; Ex. C6 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 22), The complainant confirmed, on at least four separate occasions that the conduct occurred before last Christmas, The preliminary hearing testimony placed events in "the middle of fifth grade," a "medium time before" the complainant's Arizona Christmas trip. (Ex. D4 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 12; Ex. D15 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 63) at trial B.J. herself clarified her seemingly ambiguous "Before they were married" placement representing late 2018 when answering about Shyanne Pike (Ex. F33 - Trial Tr. II at 292; Ex. F4 - Trial Tr. II at 293) No witness at any stage placed the conduct outside that window. Under *Napue*, 360 U.S. at 269, and *Miller v. Pate*, 386 U.S. 1, 7 (1967), the State's knowing misrepresentation of its own record — offered specifically to erase the defense's timeline-impossibility theory — is a free-standing due process violation. The prejudice is that the false statement foreclosed the jury's consideration of the only defense theory available: that the conduct could not have occurred as alleged because Petitioner was not present during the only window ever identified.

**2. Vouching for Judicially Impeached Witnesses:** The court instructed the jury that it could find both the complainant and Jeffrey Jackson "not believable or truthful." (Ex. K6–K7) The prosecutor nonetheless told the jury, in closing: "We know she's telling the truth" (Ex. H4 - Trial Tr. IV at 78; Ex. H5 - Trial Tr. IV at 81), "She has absolutely no motive to make this up" (Ex. H6 - Trial Tr. IV at 61), "She experienced it. That's not a detail you can make up… It happened to her" (Ex. H7 - Trial Tr. IV at 85) — twenty-one such assertions across closing for the complainant alone. As to Jackson, the sole witness to the initial disclosure: "we know he's not lying" and "He knows those things for sure because we know he's not lying." (Ex. H17 - Trial Tr. IV at 65)

Under *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 18 (1985), a prosecutor may not express personal belief in a witness's credibility. Under *United States v. Causey*, 834 F.2d 1277, 1285 (6th Cir. 1988), the test is whether the jury could reasonably understand the prosecutor to be vouching personally — and "we know she's telling the truth" satisfies that test on its face. The vouching was particularly egregious because it directly contradicted the court's own instructions: the judge had told the jury these witnesses "Had made statements inconsistent with their testimony" and could be found not credible; the prosecutor told the jury they were telling the truth and the government knew it. That defense counsel attacked credibility does not open the door to vouching. *People v. Rodriguez*, 9 Cal. 5th 474 (2020). The prejudice is that the jury was told, by the government's representative, to disregard the court's own instruction and trust the prosecutor's personal assurance of veracity, Repeatedly.

**3. Burden-Shifting Argument:** Responding to the defense's reasonable-doubt theory grounded in investigative deficiencies, the prosecutor told the jury: "If you were unhappy with that investigation, the remedy to that is to not say, okay, we're just going to find him not guilty… Complain to the chief, Chief Foster. We'll give you his phone number." (Ex. H9 - Trial Tr. IV at 67) This argument did not merely respond to the defense; it affirmatively told the jury that acquittal was the wrong response to the State's own investigative failures. Under *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510, 520 (1979), and *Francis v. Franklin*, 471 U.S. 307, 313 (1985), any argument with the effect of relieving the State of its burden of persuasion violates due process. The prejudice is that the jury was redirected from its constitutional function — determining whether the State had met its burden — to an administrative remedy that had nothing to do with guilt or innocence.

**4. Inflammatory Epithets Before Verdict:** Before any verdict, the prosecutor repeatedly called Petitioner "abuser," "child molester" (Ex. H10 - Trial Tr. IV at 73; Ex. H11 - Trial Tr. IV at 93), and "sex offender" (Ex. H12 - Trial Tr. IV at 88), framing the case as a binary moral choice: "He made a choice to be stepdad or child molester, and he crossed that line." (Ex. H11 - Trial Tr. IV at 93) Under *People v. Fletcher*, 509 N.E.2d 625, 631 (Ill. App. Ct. 1987), and *Kellogg v. Skon*, No. 97-3237, 1999 WL 342332, at 3 (8th Cir. May 25, 1999), epithets of this kind compel the jury to react to the grossness of the accusation rather than weigh whether Petitioner in fact committed it. In a case with no physical evidence and no corroboration, the resort to these terms was not color — it was a substitute for proof. The prejudice is that the jury was invited to convict based on the label, not the evidence.

**5. Golden Rule Arguments:** During voir dire, the prosecutor asked a female juror to describe her "last consensual sexual encounter," remarking afterward, "I was uncomfortable for you," and invited the panel to imagine testifying about such details "with 12 people in the box staring at you." (Ex. E1 - Trial Tr. I at 138) This exercise had no evidentiary purpose other than to manufacture sympathetic identification with the complainant before trial began. In closing, she urged jurors to "imagine" the complainant's experience: "in her little mind, when someone is touching her vagina… something bad has happened." (Ex. H13 - Trial Tr. IV at 23) Under *United States v. Palma*, 473 F.3d 899, 902 (8th Cir. 2007), golden rule arguments are "universally condemned" because they substitute personal, emotional identification for neutral evaluation. The prejudice is that in a case where the defense theory was timeline impossibility — not that the complainant was lying about her subjective experience — the prosecutor redirected the jury to an emotional exercise that bypassed the actual defense entirely.

**6. Misstatement of Credibility Law:** The prosecutor told the jury it should not expect "every single word to be the same… except from a seasoned liar" (Ex. H15 - Trial Tr. IV at 32), and separately argued that the complainant's sensory detail was itself proof of truthfulness because "that's not a detail you can make up." (Ex. H14 - Trial Tr. IV at 85) This inverted ordinary credibility analysis into a fabricated rule: consistency signals fabrication, and any level of detail proves veracity. Under *United States v. Eyster*, 948 F.2d 1196, 1201 (11th Cir. 1991), a prosecutor may not supply the jury with an invented credibility standard. The prejudice is that the jury was instructed — by the prosecutor, not the court — to distrust the very indicia of reliability that credibility determinations ordinarily rest on, in a case where credibility was the entirety of the State's case.

**7. Trivializing Reasonable Doubt and Preconditioning the Venire:** Before a single witness was sworn, the prosecutor spent roughly two hours of voir dire conditioning the panel: comparing the elements to "granny's potato soup" (Ex. E2 - Trial Tr. I at 119); falsely telling jurors "you're not going to get an instruction about what beyond a reasonable doubt means" (Ex. E6 - Trial Tr. I at 186); asking whether jurors believed sexual-abuse cases "usually" have witnesses or DNA evidence (Ex. E8 - Trial Tr. I at 130; Ex. E9 - Trial Tr. I at 131); and asking whether the State could prove its case "through the word of a child alone." (Ex. E7 - Trial Tr. I at 160) Under *Sandstrom*, 442 U.S. at 520, and *United States v. Velazquez*, No. 19-50158, 2021 WL 2494909, at 5 (9th Cir. June 23, 2021), this was not preliminary color but structural preconditioning of the fact-finder. The prejudice is that the jury was seated — and deliberated — with a diluted, trivialized understanding of the Constitution's central protection.

**8. Improper "Burden-Met" Declarations:** Walking through each element of both counts in closing, the prosecutor declared outright: "The State of Oklahoma has met their burden of proof as it relates to that first element" (Ex. H3 - Trial Tr. IV at 19); "Right there. Right there we've met that element" and materially identical declarations for the second through sixth elements of Count I and all three elements of Count II — at least eight deliberate declarations that the State had already proven its case. Under *Young*, 470 U.S. at 18, and *United States v. Modica*, 663 F.2d 1173, 1178–79 (2d Cir. 1981), a prosecutor may not tell a jury the government has already met its burden. The prejudice is that the jury was told, element by element, that its deliberative function was complete — that the State had already done its job, and the jury's only remaining task was to ratify the prosecutor's conclusions.

**9. Cumulative Effect of Pervasive Prosecutorial Misconduct:** The full record — catalogued in the Post-Convictions 107-page appendix — documents over 175 improper statements during voir dire and over 80 during closing. Under *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935), and *Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974), the cumulative effect — not each instance in isolation — determines whether a trial remained fundamentally fair. Where misconduct spans voir dire through closing and is "pronounced and persistent," *United States v. Darden*, 688 F.3d 382, 391 (8th Cir. 2012) (Bright, J., dissenting), no single curative instruction — and none was given here — can restore the fairness that was lost. The prejudice is the trial itself.

**SECTION C: MULTIPLE EGREGIOUS TRIAL COURT ERRORS VIOLATED PETITIONER'S RIGHTS UNDER THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT OF THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION**

**1. No Adequate Waiver-of-Right-to-Testify Colloquy:** A criminal defendant's right to testify in his own defense is a right "personal to the defendant," rooted in the Fifth, Sixth, and Fourteenth Amendments. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 51–53 (1987). Like other constitutional rights, it may be waived only if the waiver is "knowing and voluntary." *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938); see also *Hunter v. United States*, 588 A.2d 680, 684 (D.C. 1991) (applying the *Johnson* standard). Because the right is "fundamental," only the defendant may waive it, and a court may not accept a waiver inferred from silence or from counsel's unilateral declaration. *Roelker v. People*, 804 P.2d 1336, 1338 (Colo. 1991) (dissent); *Eltabech v. Hopkins*, 997 F.2d 386, 387 (8th Cir. 1993). Where "the trial judge has reason to believe that defense counsel is frustrating the defendant's desire to testify," the judge must "inquire discreetly into the disagreement and ensure that constitutional rights are not suppressed wrongly." *United States v. Van De Walker*, 141 F.3d 1451, 1453 n.2 (11th Cir. 1998); see *United States v. Pennycooke*, 65 F.3d 9, 12 (3d Cir. 1995) (same).

Here, the waiver was tendered mid-trial, after years of preparation, at a whispered bench conference with the jury present, (Ex. L2 - Partial Proc. at 3) with the prosecutor interrupting to complain about volume. Co-counsel—the same counsel accused of coercion—conducted the entire colloquy with leading questions. The court never asked Petitioner whether he understood his right, whether he had been pressured, or why a planned defense was being abandoned. Under *Van De Walker* and *Pennycooke* these were precisely the "exceptional circumstances" requiring independent judicial inquiry. The court's failure to ask a single question was not a neutral omission; it was an abdication of the "serious and weighty responsibility" to ensure a "knowing and voluntary" waiver. *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 465.

**2. Failure to Correct the Prosecutor's Known-False Statement:** It is a "due process violation" for the prosecution to "knowingly use false evidence," and the prosecutor has an affirmative duty to "correct testimony he knows to be false and elicit the truth." *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264, 269 (1959); *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150, 153–54 (1972); *United States v. Langston*, 970 F.2d 692, 696 (10th Cir. 1992). A prosecutor's deliberate misstatement of the evidence during closing argument can deprive a defendant of a fair trial if it "infect\[s\] the trial with unfairness." *Darden v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 168, 181 (1986).

The prosecutor told the jury, "No one ever said 2018," flatly contradicting the police report and prior testimony the court had received. (Ex. H2 - Trial Tr. IV at 96) The court knew the statement was false yet said nothing. The uncorrected falsehood concealed the timeline impossibility—the defense's central theory—from the jury. Under *Napue* and *Langston*, the State's duty to correct was absolute; the court's silence compounded the violation.

**3. No Limiting Instruction for Ferguson's Res Gestae Testimony:** The Supreme Court has instructed that state evidentiary rulings violate due process only if they render the trial "fundamentally unfair." *Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62, 72–73 (1991). This standard is satisfied when the admission of other-acts evidence is so prejudicial and so lacking in procedural safeguards that the jury is permitted to convict based on propensity rather than proof of the charged offense. *Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 691–92 (1988) (emphasizing the trial court's gatekeeping function under Rule 404(b) to prevent unfair prejudice). Where evidence admitted without a limiting instruction "permitted the jury to misuse the evidence" and convict based on "implied propensity," the error is not merely evidentiary; it is constitutional. *Rojas v. People*, No. 20SC399, 2022 WL 528998, at 14 (Colo. Feb. 22, 2022) (state case applying federal constitutional standard); see also *People v. Agado*, 964 P.2d 565, 567 (Colo. Ct. App. 1998) (Vogt, J., specially concurring).

The court admitted Ferguson's highly prejudicial testimony as res gestae and refused a limiting instruction under a blanket, category-based rule that such evidence "would not qualify or necessitate a separate instruction, as would typical Burks evidence." The jury heard allegations of unsolicited photographs and inappropriate sexual comments with no guidance restricting its use. Under *Estelle*, a trial becomes fundamentally unfair when the court abdicates its gatekeeping role and allows the jury to convict based on propensity. The absence of any limiting instruction here crossed that constitutional threshold.

**4. Prejudgment of the New-Trial Motion:** "A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process." *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955). A defendant is entitled to a hearing before "an impartial decisionmaker who does not prejudge the evidence and who cannot say … how he would assess evidence he has not yet seen." *Gonzales v. Johnson*, 994 F. Supp. 759, 764 (N.D. Tex. 1997). A judge who announces a predisposition before hearing argument has violated the "neutral, detached" posture due process requires. *Dyas v. Lockhart*, 705 F.2d 993, 995 (8th Cir. 1983); see also *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 532–33 (1927). The Supreme Court has recognized that "no man can be a judge in his own case" and that recusal is constitutionally required wherever "the probability of actual bias" is too high to tolerate. *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868, 886 (2009).

The court announced, "I'm not inclined to grant a new trial … I don't see a legal basis for granting that relief," (Ex. I7 - Sent. Tr. at 4) before hearing argument or evidence. Only then did it permit counsel to argue and Petitioner to testify. Under *Murchison* and *Gonzales* this was not a preliminary leaning; it was a prejudgment that infected the entire proceeding and denied Petitioner the "fair tribunal" the Constitution guarantees.

**5. Sentencing on a Prosecutor-Authored, Inaccurate PSI:** A criminal defendant has a due process interest in the sentencing stage, and this right "is abridged when a defendant is sentenced on the basis of 'materially untrue' statements." *Townsend v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 736, 741 (1948). A defendant is denied due process when sentenced based on "information which he had no opportunity to deny or explain." *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 362 (1977). "Where there are disputed facts material to the sentencing decision, the district court must cause the record to reflect its resolution thereof." *United States v. Hart*, 922 F.2d 613, 617 (10th Cir. 1990).

The PSI here was not a neutral document; it was a prosecutorial instrument. The "Victim's Version" was dictated by the prosecutor, at the bottom of page 1 it shows "this officer was contacted by Ms. Austin of the District Attorney's Office on August 8, 2022 who stated that the victim's version from the Affidavit of Probable Cause would be sufficient information for the court": the "Defendant's Version" stated it "was not requested by the court"; the forensic interview was listed under the wrong date; and the uncharged Ferguson allegations were recited as established fact. The court expressly relied on this corrupted report. Under *Townsend* and *Gardner*, a sentence predicated on a PSI that the defendant had no opportunity to correct—and that his own prosecutor authored—is constitutionally infirm. (Ex. I5 - PSI)

**6. § 2803.1 Hearing Proceeded on an Uncorrected, Admittedly False Notice:** An indictment or charging document must provide "fair notice of the charges" with "reasonable particularity to allow the defendant to prepare a defense." *Russell v. United States*, 369 U.S. 749, 763–64 (1962). A defendant's right "to be informed of the nature of the evidence against him is fundamental to due process." *Wardius v. Oregon*, 412 U.S. 470, 474 (1973). Where the State provides materially false information in a notice filing, it deprives the accused of the opportunity to confront and challenge the evidence. *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 682 (1985).

The State filed a notice alleging disclosure at "grandmother's house"—a scenario the prosecutor conceded was "an error on my part on a cut and paste to a previous case." The court never struck the false notice or required a corrected one. The defense was forced to challenge hearsay based on a notice the State admitted was fictional. Under *Russell* and *Wardius*, proceeding on a knowingly false notice deprived Petitioner of the fair notice required to prepare his defense. (Ex. K4)

**7. Five-Month Deferral of the § 2803.1 Reliability Ruling:** The Confrontation Clause requires that gatekeeping determinations regarding the reliability of testimonial hearsay occur before the evidence is presented to the jury, to preserve the opportunity for "meaningful adversarial testing." *Idaho v. Wright*, 497 U.S. 805, 822 (1990); *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 68 (2004). "Deferring admissibility rulings until mid-trial deprives counsel of the ability to adjust strategy and undermines structural fairness." *Myatt v. Hannigan*, 910 F.2d 680, 685 (10th Cir. 1990)

The court deferred its ruling for five months—from January 21, 2022, until the third day of trial. (Ex. G7 - Trial Tr. III at 5) By then, defense counsel had delivered an opening statement and cross-examined multiple witnesses without knowing whether the State's central exhibit would be admitted. Under *Myatt* and *Crawford*, this uncertainty fundamentally impaired the right to present a defense. The five-month deferral transformed the reliability hearing from a gatekeeping function into an afterthought.

**8. A Single Conclusory Sentence in Place of a Reliability Finding:** The admission of child hearsay under the Confrontation Clause requires "particularized guarantees of trustworthiness" drawn from "the totality of circumstances that surround the making of the statement and that render the declarant particularly worthy of belief." *Idaho v. Wright*, 497 U.S. 805, 819 (1990). This inquiry must be based on the circumstances surrounding the statement's making; it "cannot be based on other corroborating evidence." *Id.* at 822. A conclusory finding that an interview "comports with statutory requirements" is not a sufficient reliability determination under the Confrontation Clause.

Here, the court's entire analysis was that the interview "comport\[ed\] with the statutory requirements." (Ex. G7 - Trial Tr. III at 5) The court made no specific findings tied to the video's contents, identified no "particularized guarantees of trustworthiness," and could not even recall which proceeding produced the disk. Under *Wright*, a bare conclusion without analysis of the circumstances rendering the child's statement trustworthy is constitutionally inadequate.

**9. Admission of a Different, Unauthenticated Disk:** The Confrontation Clause requires strict adherence to procedural safeguards, including authentication, before testimonial hearsay is admitted. *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 68 (2004). "The requirement of authentication or identification as a condition precedent to admissibility is satisfied by evidence sufficient to support a finding that the matter in question is what its proponent claims." *United States v. Ford*, 279 F. App'x 68, 69 (2d Cir. 2008) (quoting Fed. R. Evid. 901(a)). The admission of unauthenticated testimonial evidence violates the Confrontation Clause. *United States v. Jackson*, 636 F.3d 687, 691 (5th Cir. 2010).

The court reviewed and admitted State's Exhibit 1 (relabeled Court's Exhibit 1). (Ex. G7 - Trial Tr. III at 5) The jury viewed State's Exhibit 6— (Ex. G2 - Trial Tr. III at 122) a different, edited disk that had never been separately authenticated, bore no witness initials, and was never subjected to judicial gatekeeping. Under *Jackson* and *Ford*, admitting an unauthenticated substitute exhibit for the one the court vetted violates foundational evidentiary requirements and the Confrontation Clause. The jury was shown evidence that had never been subjected to judicial review.

**10. Cumulative Error / Synergistic Effect:** Even if any single error were harmless standing alone, their cumulative, "synergistic effect" independently requires vacatur. *Cargle v. Mullin*, 317 F.3d 1196, 1206–07 (10th Cir. 2003). "To deny cumulative-error consideration of claims unless they have first satisfied their individual substantive standards for actionable prejudice would render the cumulative error inquiry meaningless." *Malicoat v. Mullin*, 426 F.3d 1241, 1252 (10th Cir. 2005) (quoting *Cargle*, 317 F.3d at 1207). "A cumulative-error analysis merely aggregates all the errors that individually have been found to be harmless, and … analyzes whether their cumulative effect on the outcome of the trial is such that collectively they can no longer be determined to be harmless." *Darks v. Gibson*, 327 F.3d 1001, 1008 (10th Cir. 2003).

Here, the errors were not "isolated, insular errors scattered randomly throughout the proceedings." *Cargle*, 317 F.3d at 1221. They had "an inherent synergistic effect" at every critical juncture: the admission of the central exhibit (Grounds 6–9), the waiver of the right to testify (Ground 1), the uncorrected falsehood (Ground 2), the prejudicial other-acts evidence (Ground 3), the prejudged motion (Ground 4), and the corrupted sentencing (Ground 5). Taken together, they show a trial court that abdicated its gatekeeping and supervisory role, leaving Petitioner with a proceeding that was fundamentally unfair in its totality.

**SECTION D: ARBITRARY EXPANSION OF THE CHARGING WINDOW DESPITE A CONSISTENT LATE-2018 TIMELINE**

An indictment must provide fair notice of the charges and allege the offense with reasonable particularity of time, place, and circumstances. *Russell v. United States*, 369 U.S. 749, 763–64 (1962); *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948). The Tenth Circuit has held that a charging instrument must apprise the defendant of the specific facts he must meet. *United States v. Rivera*, 837 F.2d 906 (10th Cir. 1988). When time is an essential ingredient of the offense — such as where the defense turns on an alibi — a charging window that is arbitrarily broadened in the face of contrary evidence is unconstitutional.

Every stage of this case fixed the same timeframe. The July 14, 2019 police report documented the occurrence from 8:00 a.m. on November 29, 2018 through 11:00 p.m. on December 25, 2018. (Ex. A1–A2) The July 22, 2019 forensic interview placed it "before they were married." (Ex. B3 - FI Tr. at 19:51) At the July 29, 2019 protective order hearing, asked by the court to choose between days, weeks, months, or years ago, the complainant answered "months ago" (Ex. C18 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 11), and repeatedly testified the events occurred "before last Christmas" and "before Christmas." (Ex. C3 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 13; Ex. C5 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 16; Ex. C6 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 22) She confirmed it "never happened outside of that timeframe" (Ex. C17 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 32) and that it was "closer to beginning of School / Still cold out." (Ex. C17 - PO Hr'g Tr. at 32) At the January 15, 2020 preliminary hearing, she placed the events "a long time after moving in," in the "middle of fifth grade," "certainly not" fourth grade, and "medium time" before going to her father's for Christmas — the only such visit being December 2018. (Ex. D4 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 12; Ex. D5 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 13; Ex. D9 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 44; Ex. D10 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 45; Ex. D21 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 49; Ex. D15 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 63) She placed her mother's marriage to Petitioner as occurring roughly a year and a half after they moved into the home and did not know whether they were married in December 2018. (Ex. D19 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 43; Ex. D13 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 50) At trial on June 22, 2022, she clarified that "before they got married" referred to a late-2018 separation — Petitioner had broken up with her mother, was seeing Shyanne Pike, and then got back together before eventually divorcing. (Ex. F4 - Trial Tr. II at 293)

At no point, across five proceedings and over three years, did the complainant place the events in any other timeframe.

The State obtained its nearly two-year charging window not from any evidence of conduct outside that timeframe, but solely because the complainant, a child, could not name an exact calendar date. At the close of the preliminary hearing, the State moved to amend the Information from its original June 1, 2018–January 2019 window to September 1, 2017–June 1, 2019, and to add an entirely new second count carrying the identical, undifferentiated window — over defense counsel's objection that there was no testimony supporting conduct anywhere near the expanded dates. (Ex. D22 - Prelim. Hr'g Tr. at 70) That the second count was pled with the same unparticularized two-year range as the first compounds the defect: where a State charges multiple counts using generic, interchangeable language and an identical broad window, it fails to place the defendant on notice of what distinguishes one count from another. *See Valentine v. Konteh*, 395 F.3d 626, 634 (6th Cir. 2005).

The State exploited a child's inability to state a specific date to expand the window far beyond the only period she ever alleged, defeating Petitioner's ability to present the alibi defense set out in Ground One. Every proceeding produced a temporal anchor pointing to December 2018; none produced any evidence supporting the seven-months-earlier or year-later dates the amended window added. The State maintained the arbitrary window notwithstanding. The procurement of a two-year charging window based on a child's inability to specify a date, while ignoring her consistent placement of the events in December 2018, violated due process. The conviction cannot stand on a charging instrument obtained this way.

**SECTION E: POST-CONVICTION PROCEEDINGS — FREESTANDING DUE PROCESS VIOLATIONS**

Petitioner recognizes the Tenth Circuit has more often resolved challenges to state post-conviction proceedings by holding them non-cognizable outright, *Sellers v. Ward*, 135 F.3d 1333, 1339 (10th Cir. 1998); *Steele v. Young*, 11 F.3d 1518, 1524 (10th Cir. 1993), and raises the six grounds below in the alternative and for preservation.

Under *District Attorney's Office v. Osborne*, 557 U.S. 52, 69 (2009), when a state chooses to create a post-conviction remedy, the Due Process Clause requires that the procedure not "offend some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental," or "transgress any recognized principle of fundamental fairness in operation." *Id.* The Second Circuit applied this exact framework in *Newton v. City of New York*, 779 F.3d 140, 152 (2d Cir. 2015), holding that "fundamental fairness requires that once a state creates a statutory right and puts procedures in place to protect that right, those procedures must comport with due process in application." Where a state's post-conviction scheme is "so inadequate as to nullify" the statutory procedures in practice, the arbitrary application itself violates the Fourteenth Amendment. *Id.* at 149. Oklahoma created a post-conviction remedy in 22 O.S. § 1084. Its operation here violated due process at every stage.

**1. Verbatim Adoption of the State's Brief:** A post-conviction order that adopts the prosecution's submission verbatim is not a reasoned adjudication; it is a rubber stamp that strips the proceeding of any judicial character and renders the state-created remedy illusory. *Newton v. City of New York*, 779 F.3d at 149 (holding that an "as-applied" challenge succeeds when municipal practice is "so inadequate as to nullify" state procedures). Under *Osborne*, 557 U.S. at 69, a state-created right "beget\[s\] … rights to procedures essential to the realization of the parent right." The February 13, 2026 order was a 95% side-by-side match with the State's seven-page response, repeating even its demonstrably false factual assertions. Because the court performed no independent analysis, Petitioner was denied the "procedures essential to the realization" of Oklahoma's post-conviction remedy. This "transgress\[ed\] any recognized principle of fundamental fairness in operation." *Id.*

**2. Factual Errors Contradicted by the Record:** When a state post-conviction court makes findings that are the opposite of the record before it, the fact-finding procedure is not merely inaccurate; it is fundamentally unfair. *Brumfield v. Cain*, 135 S. Ct. 2269, 2278 (2015). The district court found that Petitioner's claims "were, or could have been, raised on direct appeal" and were "acknowledged as waived." Both findings are affirmatively contradicted by the application itself, which invoked ineffective assistance of appellate counsel as a statutory basis for post-conviction relief—a claim that by definition cannot be raised on direct appeal. Under *Osborne*, 557 U.S. at 69, procedures that produce arbitrary, record-contradictory results are not "adequate to vindicate the substantive rights provided." The court's false findings nullified the statutory scheme and violated due process.

**3. Dismissal of Documented Claims as "Conclusory":** A blanket dismissal of 171 exhibits and a 107-page appendix as "conclusory," without addressing a single exhibit or quoting a single transcript page, is not judicial review; it is a refusal to engage with the evidence, rendering the post-conviction proceeding a sham. *Newton v. City of New York*, 779 F.3d at 152 ("fundamental fairness requires that once a state creates a statutory right and puts procedures in place to protect that right, those procedures must comport with due process in application"). Under *Oken v. Warden, MSP*, 233 F.3d 86, 91 (1st Cir. 2000), "due process requirements of fundamental fairness apply to state post-conviction proceedings." Where the court refuses to review uncontested documentary evidence—lease agreements, text messages, travel records, and sworn transcripts—it applies a procedure that "offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Osborne*, 557 U.S. at 69 (internal quotation marks omitted).

**4. Failure to Hold an Evidentiary Hearing:** A state post-conviction proceeding that refuses to hold a hearing on disputed material facts—despite a statutory mandate to do so—fails the "fundamental fairness" test the Due Process Clause imposes on all state-created remedial schemes. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963). *Newton v. City of New York*, 779 F.3d at 149, recognized that an "as-applied" challenge to state post-conviction procedures succeeds when the petitioner "diligently and repeatedly tried the State's procedures" and showed them to fail in practice. Here, the State contested no material fact; it argued only procedural default. Yet the court refused to hold a hearing on claims involving a coerced waiver, a destroyed body-camera recording, and an unauthenticated substitute disk. By denying Petitioner the hearing Oklahoma law requires, the court rendered the statutory remedy "fundamentally inadequate" and violated due process.

**5. Failure to Disqualify Judge Balkman:** Due process requires not merely the absence of actual bias, but an adjudicator who is not "a judge in his own case." *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955). The Supreme Court reinforced this in *Williams v. Pennsylvania*, 136 S. Ct. 1899, 1905 (2016), holding that due process is violated when a judge adjudicates a case in which he previously had "a significant, personal involvement as a prosecutor." The principle is no less applicable when the judge adjudicates claims challenging his own trial conduct: his waiver colloquy, his evidentiary rulings, his sentencing, and his refusal to correct a prosecutor's false statement. Under *Osborne*, 557 U.S. at 69, a state-created remedy must not "transgress any recognized principle of fundamental fairness in operation." A post-conviction proceeding before the very judge whose conduct is challenged—followed nine days later by a verbatim adoption of the prosecution's brief—is the definition of a procedure that offends "fundamental fairness."

**6. Failure to Issue Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law:** A post-conviction order that contains zero original findings and zero original conclusions is not a judicial decision; it is a nullity. Under *Wilson v. Sellers*, 138 S. Ct. 1188, 1192 (2018), a federal court must "look through" an unexplained state-court decision to the last reasoned decision below. But where the lower court's order is a verbatim copy of the State's brief, there is no "relevant rationale" to look through to. *Newton v. City of New York*, 779 F.3d at 149, holds that a state's post-conviction procedures must be "adequate" in operation. Oklahoma law itself mandates "specific findings of fact" and "conclusions of law." 22 O.S. § 1084; OCCA Rule 5.2(A). The total failure to comply—producing an order with no independent reasoning—meant the state-court adjudication was not a proceeding at all. Under *Osborne* a state-created remedy administered through a procedure this empty violates the Due Process Clause.

<center><h4>GROUND FOUR: THE CUMULATIVE EFFECT OF TRIAL COUNSEL, APPELLATE COUNSEL, PROSECUTORIAL, AND JUDICIAL ERRORS DENIED PETITIONER A FUNDAMENTALLY FAIR TRIAL IN VIOLATION OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT</b></center>

Even if this Court were to conclude that no single ground above independently warrants relief, their cumulative effect does. "A cumulative-error analysis aggregates all errors found to be harmless and analyzes whether their cumulative effect on the outcome of the trial is such that collectively they can no longer be determined to be harmless." *Cargle v. Mullin*, 317 F.3d 1196, 1207 (10th Cir. 2003).

This case presents an unusually complete record for that analysis. The jury that convicted Petitioner: never heard the protective order hearing transcript, in which the complainant confirmed fourteen separate times — across multiple temporal anchors — that the conduct occurred in late 2018; never heard the preliminary hearing transcript, in which the same timeline was confirmed again, and in which Tiffany Norton testified Petitioner "moved out during the summer of '18" and returned "at the end of December in '18"; never saw the lease, the moving photographs, the text messages, the Arizona travel records, or the contemporaneous behavioral evidence spanning the full charging window; never learned that the State's own forensic interviewer had acknowledged the complainant "clearly had been told or informed as to what to expect"; never learned that the lead detective's central claim — that he could not interview the complainant — was false; never learned that the corroborating witness's employment timeline was fabricated by a full year; never learned that the body-worn camera recording of the original disclosure — documenting the couch location, the denial of the second count, and the December 2018 timeframe — had been destroyed while non-exculpatory evidence from the same night was preserved; and never learned that the disk they watched was not the disk the court had reviewed.

The jury that convicted Petitioner was, during voir dire, conditioned through 185 leading questions to accept the complainant's word alone, to excuse the absence of physical evidence, and to apply a standard of proof trivialized to a soup recipe. During closing, the jury was told — without objection — that the State had "met its burden" on every element, that the complainant was "telling the truth," that her father was "not lying," that "no one ever said 2018," and that Petitioner was a "child molester." The jury included a member whose brother had been convicted of the identical offense and whose family friend had committed suicide because of it.

The defendant the jury convicted never testified — not because he chose not to, but because his lawyer told him during lunch on the last day of trial that he "did not need to testify," and then conducted a whispered waiver colloquy with no judicial inquiry. At the sentencing hearing where that decision could have been challenged, the same lawyer told the court his client's sworn account was "factually" false.

And when these errors were presented to the state courts for correction: the district court copied the prosecution's brief verbatim, including its falsehoods, without reviewing a single exhibit; the trial judge ruled on claims challenging his own conduct after refusing recusal; and the OCCA affirmed on a 45-page record that could not have contained the evidence, claiming a "thorough review."

The aggregation of the errors set forth above—the actual innocence evidence that was never presented, the multiple instances of ineffective assistance of trial and appellate counsel, the prosecutorial misconduct and presentation of false evidence, and the trial court's abdication of its gatekeeping responsibilities—had the synergistic effect of denying Petitioner a fundamentally fair trial. *Cargle v. Mullin*, 317 F.3d 1196, 1207 (10th Cir. 2003). The state courts never evaluated the cumulative impact of these errors because they either failed to recognize the claims or refused to consider the supporting evidence. In the absence of any merits adjudication, this Court must analyze cumulative error de novo. When all errors are considered together, no confidence can be placed in the jury's verdict. The writ of habeas corpus must issue.
